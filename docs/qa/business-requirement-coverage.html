What this reviews, and against what

A different question from the last review — and a harder one.

The previous review asked "is each story built and tested?" This one asks "if every story on this board were delivered perfectly, would the bank's problem be solved?" A backlog can be fully implemented, fully tested, and still miss the point.

The source of truth

The business requirements are the client brief in CLAUDE.md — the problem statement as given. It states four things a BA can hold the backlog against: the business case (why this exists), the functional requirements (what the platform must do), the non-negotiable constraints, and the actors.

One thing to keep in mind throughout

The brief says the problem statement is "deliberately incomplete — expansion is graded." So a gap between the brief and the backlog is not automatically a failure. The question for each gap is: was it filled deliberately, filled accidentally, or left open without anyone deciding? The third is the only one that is actually a problem — and this review separates them.

The business case

Four stated problems. The backlog solves two of them well, one implicitly, and does not address one at all.

Runs on email/Word/Excel/SharePoint. Assessment takes 15–20 business days. Two analysts can reach different conclusions on the same change. Reconstructing the reasoning behind a past rating (for examiners) is slow and incomplete.
BCThe stated painCovered byRating
BC-1 Fragmented tooling — email, Word, Excel, SharePoint Epics 1–10 deliver one governed platform end to end. Epic 14 replaces the "go ask another system" step Served
BC-2 15–20 business day cycle Nothing. No story sets a target, measures elapsed time against one, escalates a breach, or reports on throughput Not served
BC-3 Two analysts reach different conclusions Helped implicitly by AI category proposal and a deterministic scoring formula — but no story names consistency as a goal or measures it Partial
BC-4 Examiner reconstruction is slow and incomplete US-9.1 (full history), US-9.2 (immutability), US-9.3 (examiner-ready export) Served
BC-2 is the headline business benefit, and nothing measures it

"15–20 business days" is the number a sponsor would quote to justify funding this platform. The backlog contains no target, no service level, no escalation, and no reporting against it. I confirmed this in the data model: there is no due_date, sla, target_date or cycle_time anywhere in the schema.

The system does show days elapsed on a Product Owner's request list — but that is a number on a screen with nothing to compare it to. At the end of this project nobody will be able to say whether the platform made assessments faster, because nothing records what "faster" would mean.

BC-3 is served by accident rather than by design

Two mechanisms genuinely reduce inconsistency: every assessment starts from the same AI-proposed categories against the same citable framework, and the score is a fixed formula rather than a judgement. Both are real, and both are good design.

But no story says "analysts should reach consistent conclusions", so nothing verifies it and nothing protects it. If someone later made scoring configurable per analyst, no acceptance criterion anywhere would object. Consistency is currently an emergent property, not a requirement — and emergent properties are the ones that quietly disappear.

Worth saying plainly

Ten out of ten functional requirements have a home, and the mapping is one-to-one with the brief's own list. The four decision outcomes the brief names — approved, rejected, deferred, approved-with-conditions — appear verbatim as database constraints. That is textbook traceability from a client brief to a working system, and it is the single strongest thing about this backlog.

The AI requirement, specifically

The brief is precise about where AI belongs: "mapping the request to risk categories, finding relevant policy, extracting structure from submitted documents, and drafting the assessment narrative." Four named touchpoints.

TouchpointEpicImplemented as
Map to risk categories2AI grounded against a fixed category list
Find relevant policy3Deterministic search — a deliberate deviation
Extract from documents4AI with confidence and source excerpt
Draft the narrative5AI with a fabrication guard

Epic 3 is the interesting one: the brief asked for AI, the team chose full-text search instead, and documented why — ranking indexed text is a solved problem, and an LLM would add latency, cost and nondeterminism for no accuracy gain. That is a deviation from the brief, and a good one — but it is a decision the client should be told about rather than discover.

A BA observation on NC-4

The override rule is the most widely spread requirement in the brief — it appears in five separate stories across four epics, and the brief calls it first-class. But it exists only as a repeated acceptance criterion; no single story owns it as a cross-cutting rule.

That is why it ended up the least-verified rule in the product (from the previous review, only one of the five has a test that a blank reason is refused). A requirement spread across five stories and owned by none is the classic way a non-negotiable quietly becomes optional.

Business gaps

Seven things the business needs that no story currently delivers. Ordered by business consequence, not effort.

BG-1HighBusiness case

Nothing measures the cycle time the project exists to reduce

No target, no service level, no escalation on a breach, no throughput reporting. Confirmed absent from the data model — no due_date, sla, target_date or cycle_time column exists.

The consequence is not technical. At the end of this project, no one can demonstrate the platform made anything faster — which is the benefit the business asked for.

Needs
One story: a target cycle time per change type, elapsed time measured against it, and a simple view of requests approaching or breaching it
Effort
Low — submitted_at already exists; this is arithmetic and a screen
BG-2HighDelivery

The evaluation framework has no user story at all

The brief makes AI output quality a first-class deliverable, and the project's own success criteria weight it at 10% — equal to requirement expansion and double production readiness. An evaluation harness exists in the codebase with 12 test cases across the three AI touchpoints.

But no epic and no story on the board covers it. It is real work, it is graded, and it is invisible to anyone reading the backlog — which means nobody is accountable for finishing it, and it does not appear in any plan.

Needs
An epic with stories for: evaluating each AI touchpoint against fixed datasets, running the evaluation in the pipeline, and acting on a regression
Effort
Low to write — the work largely exists and simply is not represented
BG-3MediumBusiness case

Analyst consistency is an outcome, not a requirement

"Two analysts can reach different conclusions on the same change" is one of three stated pains. The design genuinely helps — a shared framework, a shared AI starting point, a deterministic formula — but no story names consistency, so nothing protects it from a future change.

Needs
Either a story that states it as a requirement, or an explicit note that it is served by design decisions in Epics 2 and 7 — so it is a recorded decision rather than luck
BG-4MediumFunctional

Notifications are required by three stories and built by none

US-1.3 requires the requester be notified on status change; US-8.1 requires notification when an assessment is routed; US-8.3 requires the Product Owner be told the decision and any conditions. There is no notification mechanism anywhere in the system.

This matters more than it looks: the brief's first pain is that the process runs on email. If the platform cannot tell anyone anything, users will go back to email to find out what happened — and the original problem returns through the back door.

Needs
A decision first: in scope or not? If not, remove the criteria from three stories with a stated reason. If yes, one story owns it
BG-5MediumFunctional

Escalation thresholds are specified but not implemented

US-10.2 names "escalation thresholds" among the workflow rules an analyst should be able to tune. The schema mentions EscalationThreshold.ResidualRating — as a comment. No such rule is seeded and nothing consumes one. The only workflow rule that exists is the committee quorum.

Business meaning: a very high residual risk currently follows exactly the same path as a trivial one. There is no "this one needs senior attention" mechanism, which is a normal expectation in a risk function.

BG-6MediumOrganisational

The bank has four business units; the system has none

The brief describes the client as running consumer banking, commercial banking, payments and wealth management, and says a business unit raises the change. The application data model has no concept of a business unit — a change request records who submitted it, but not which part of the bank it came from.

Consequences: no routing by business unit, no BU-specific workflow rules, and no way to answer "how much risk is the payments business generating?" — a question any FCRM function would ask. It also touches open question 2, which asked whether the framework varies by business line.

Note
Defensible to descope for a hackathon — but it should be a stated decision, since the brief introduces the business units explicitly
BG-7LowActors

"FCRM Leadership" appears as an actor with no role in the system

The brief names three user roles: Product Owner, FCRM Analyst, Risk Committee. The system implements four (adding Admin). Several stories — including US-9.2 and three raised by QA — are written from the perspective of FCRM Leadership, who has no role, no screen and no permission set.

Minor, but it means those stories have no one who can actually perform them. Either leadership is the Admin role, or the persona should be changed to one that exists.

Scope balance

Where the backlog's weight actually sits — and whether that is right.

Nearly half the backlog is about delivery rather than the business problem. On a normal bank engagement that ratio would be a serious warning sign — the client is paying for risk assessment, not for Terraform modules.

Here it is correct, and worth being able to explain

This project's success criteria weight SDLC automation across all six stages at 20% and production readiness at 5% — a quarter of the assessment is about how it was built. The infrastructure epics are not scope creep; they are a graded deliverable, and documenting them as stories is what makes that work visible.

The point worth carrying into any review conversation: this ratio is deliberate and defensible here, and would not be on a real engagement. Saying so demonstrates the judgement, rather than leaving a reviewer to wonder whether the team lost sight of the customer.

Are any stories not traceable to a requirement?

No. Every one of the 73 traces either to a functional requirement in the brief, a non-negotiable constraint, or the project's own delivery criteria. There is no scope creep — the backlog has gaps, not excess.

Verdict & actions

Would this backlog, fully delivered, solve the bank's problem?

Verdict

Substantially yes — with one significant caveat. All ten functional requirements and all five non-negotiable constraints are covered, traceably, with the brief's own vocabulary carried into the data model. A reviewer comparing the brief to the board would find the mapping clean.

The caveat is that the backlog delivers the platform the brief describes but not the improvement the brief justifies. The bank's stated pain is a 15–20 day cycle and inconsistent outcomes. The backlog would replace the tooling, and almost certainly help with both — but it contains nothing that would let anyone demonstrate either.

Still open from earlier reviews

Three questions remain unanswered and each blocks acceptance criteria: Q3 (does a scoring override need secondary sign-off), Q5 (who approves configuration changes), and Q6 (who owns database schema deployment). Q6 is the urgent one — it is an unassigned responsibility, not an open design question, and it currently blocks any deployment at all.
